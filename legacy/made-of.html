What the family version adds

For a child exploring on their own, with a parent who can see what they're learning.

Learning at home, at their own pace

Each child gets their own profile, so their progress, badges and From scratch records are theirs. Trails they can follow alone turn curiosity into a goal, and badges give them a reason to come back.

  • Child profiles: one per child, each set to their own level.
  • Badges and collections: collect all 35 elements, trace five things back to the stars, build a phone from scratch.
  • Weekly summary: what each child explored, finished and found most interesting, with a question to ask them at dinner.
  • Safe by design: no ads, no chat with strangers and no public leaderboards.

In both Family and Educator

Progress that's saved, goals to aim for, and ways to pitch the same map at the right level for each learner.

Your map of everything

Every item a learner visits is saved, so they can see how much of the map they've uncovered and pick up where they left off. The unexplored parts become something to go and find.

  • Saved exploration trees: revisit any path taken, like "shirt, thread, cotton, cotton gin".
  • Coverage: how much of the map has been discovered, overall and by tier.
  • Nearby unknowns: suggestions for the closest things not yet seen.

Goals

Learners set their own goals, or a parent or teacher sets them. Progress fills in as they explore and play, so there's always a next thing to work towards.

  • Discovery goals: meet every element, or explore all of the tree of life.
  • Game goals: beat a From scratch time, or keep a Daily link streak going.
  • Set by grown-ups: a teacher sets a goal for the class; a parent sets one for the week.

Difficulty levels

The same map, cut to the right depth. Simple hides long chains so younger students see a few familiar steps. Complex keeps every step, down to the elements, with debated links front and centre.

  • Simple: two steps upstream, the most familiar items, short From scratch targets.
  • Standard: the map as it is today.
  • Complex: full-depth chains like the microchip, with evidence on every link.
What goes into a pencil, at each level

A junior interface

A calmer screen for ages 7 to 10: big icons, short words and fewer choices at once. Facts can be read aloud, and evidence labels become plain phrases like "scientists agree" or "scientists still argue".

  • Age bands: 7 to 10, 11 to 14, and 15 and up.
  • At most four choices on each side of the bowtie.
  • Simpler wording for every fact, checked for reading level.

    Sponsor a trail or section

    Museums, science centres, societies and foundations can help keep Made Of free by sponsoring part of the map. Sponsors get a clear credit where it's relevant. They never get a say in what the map says.

    What sponsors get

    • A "made possible by" credit on a trail or a section of the map, like the tree of life or the story of the microchip.
    • A listing on the supporters page.
    • Optional: a sponsored Daily link day, themed on your subject.

    What sponsors don't get

    • Any influence over items, facts, links or evidence. Our evidence rules apply to every section, sponsored or not.

    • Sections where they have a stake. An oil company can't sponsor the oil chain, and a chipmaker can't sponsor the microchip.

    Questions

    Will the free version stay free?

    Yes. Exploring, the Daily link, From scratch and all the evidence stay free for everyone.

    Can I use Made Of in class today?

    Yes. The free version works in any classroom now. The educator version adds structure: levels, lesson plans and a shared class space.

    Is Made Of independent?

    Yes. Sponsors and paying customers never decide what goes on the map. Every link follows the same evidence rules, and debated things stay marked as debated.

    What's the difference between Family and Educator?

    Family is for a parent and a few children at home: child profiles, badges and a weekly summary. Educator is for a teacher and a whole class: lesson plans, a shared class space and a dashboard. Both include difficulty levels and the junior interface.

    When will the family and educator versions be ready?

    Both are in development, and the features above may change as we learn what parents and teachers need.

    About

    Everything comes from somewhere.

    A shirt needs thread, thread needs cotton, cotton needs a plant, and the plant needs sunlight from a star. Made Of maps those connections for 276 things, from the big bang to the internet, so you can follow any of them back to where it came from.

    How the map works

    Every item sits on a scale from raw to refined, and every link says what kind of connection it is. Most things can be made more than one way, so items can have several recipes.

      How we know

      Links carry evidence: what supports them, what argues against them, and the sources behind it. Each link gets a confidence level, so you can tell settled science from open questions. Where scientists genuinely disagree, like where life began, the map shows each idea side by side.

      The evidence was researched with the help of Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, using real published sources. It has not yet been reviewed by experts, so it may contain mistakes.

      Evidence across the map

      What's simplified

      Any map leaves things out. These are the biggest simplifications in this one:

      • It's small. 276 items stand in for millions of real things, and some chains are much deeper than others.
      • The tree of life is a single line. Fish "evolved from early animals" rather than from a specific ancestor, and real family trees branch far more.
      • Materials are generalised. "Steel" stands for hundreds of grades, and many links pick the most common way something is made.
      • Dates are first appearances, and many are approximate. Where nobody knows, the map says so.

      Inspired by

      • "I, Pencil" (1958), Leonard Read's essay pointing out that no single person knows how to make a pencil.
      • The Toaster Project, in which Thomas Thwaites tried to build a toaster from raw materials.
      • Every child who has asked "but where does that come from?" one more time.
